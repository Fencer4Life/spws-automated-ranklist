The “+” alias was refused by FTL’s server

Source: fencingtimelive.com/terms, last updated 10 March 2026 · paraphrased

What FTL’s terms say

  • The licence covers what the website shows a person, or an API that Fencing Time authorises under a separate written agreement.
  • Automated reading is forbidden: bots, scrapers or any automated means of accessing or extracting data, and getting round the site’s rate limits. The terms extend this to systematic manual copying for collection or redistribution.
  • Getting round controls is forbidden: bypassing or evading rate limiting, authentication, access control or bot detection.
  • Passing on results needs consent: making results or rankings available to third parties needs Fencing Time’s prior written consent.
  • FT watches and acts. It uses rate limiting, IP blocking and account checks to detect prohibited use, and it may suspend or end an account. An account must hold accurate information.
  • When. These terms date from 10 Mar 2026. By 31 May 2026 FTL already required a sign-in for results pages (ADR-068 found it then and taught our client to sign in).

Source: fencingtimelive.com/privacy, last updated 10 March 2026 · read 4 Nov · paraphrased

What FTL’s privacy policy adds

The privacy policy sets out what Fencing Time does with the data it holds. It grants no permission and adds no restriction on top of the terms. Four points matter here:

  • FT logs who reads. For reliability and security it records each visitor’s IP address, browser and device type, the pages visited and general usage patterns. Its login session is a cookie (our client’s connect.sid). So FT sees which machines sign in as which account and how they read: GitHub’s runners and this computer, signing in at each run, page after page. That is how a second account from the same machines can be linked to the first, which is one more reason for L1 A.
  • The results come from organisers. Placements, rankings, clubs and federation identifiers are supplied by tournament organisers, registration systems and governing bodies. The organiser’s own file is the same data at its source, which supports the “organiser’s own file” route below.
  • Nothing about third parties’ use. The policy says results are there so athletes, coaches, organisers and spectators can follow competitions. It says nothing on automated reading, APIs, account limits or reuse by others. Those are in the terms alone, so the terms decide G74.
  • One address for everything: support@fencingtime.com, for privacy questions and for deleting an account. It is the same address as the L4 letter.

Our own use of fencers’ data (names, birth years, places) is governed by the association’s own obligations, not by FT’s policy. This page does not change it.

What it means for us · G74, found 4 Nov

Our FTL route needs FTL’s written agreement

  • G74. Every FTL read of ours is automated and signed in as one account: an FTL event’s ingest, promote, the daily close, the source re-read, the hourly results sync, the URL population and the EVF sync’s FTL part. Under these terms that use needs Fencing Time’s written agreement, and so does publishing the places it reads in our ranking. We have none that I know of.
  • G63, most likely explained. The refusal asks the account holder to contact support, which fits an account suspended for prohibited use better than a counter that resets. Only FTL can confirm it.
  • Why not a new login. A second account used the same way would break the terms it accepts at sign-up. Moving to it because the first was blocked is the kind of evasion the terms name. FTL can link accounts by mailbox and by the machines that sign in, so it could block more than the new account. The four-alias rotation stays declined (L1).
  • Where it is written down today. ADR-068, the handbook’s ingestion pages and the repo’s ftl-scrape skill describe the FTL route as it runs, without these terms. They change once FTL has answered, to say what it allows; I do not rewrite them before that.

Only once FTL agrees · about five minutes · you do it

How to record the login

One piece of code reads the login, python/scrapers/ftl_auth.py, from two names: FTL_USERNAME (the account’s e-mail) and FTL_PASSWORD. They live in two places, and both must change.

  1. Use the login FTL restores or names. Sign in once with it at fencingtimelive.com in your own browser. If it is refused there, stop: no file needs changing.
  2. LOCAL: your .env. Open /Users/aleks/coding/SPWSranklist/.env in your editor (every worktree links to this one file). Change the two lines, keeping their names: 
    FTL_USERNAME='the-account@e-mail'
FTL_PASSWORD='the password'
    No spaces around =. Keep the single quotes when the password holds a space, $, # or a quote mark. Save.
  3. CI: the two repository secrets. In a terminal in the repository, run each line on its own; each asks for the value and hides what you paste: 
    gh secret set FTL_USERNAME
gh secret set FTL_PASSWORD
    Never pass the value on the command line (--body): it would stay in your shell’s history. The same works on github.com: Settings → Secrets and variables → Actions → Repository secrets. Eight workflows read these two names (ingest-event, promote, event-close, source-regression, evf-sync, populate-urls, scrape-tournament, phase5-event-runner); the hourly results sync reaches FTL through ingest-event.
  4. Tell me “login restored” and what FTL allows. I then make one read-only check on LOCAL within those conditions: a re-read of one FTL event (IMSW-2025-2026), nothing written, the result in one line. If FTL refuses, the run stops at that one attempt (G71).

What I never do

See, print, store or log the values; open .env; ask you to paste them in chat; create an account or sign in at FTL’s website. Documentation and memory name the two variables and where they live, never their values.
