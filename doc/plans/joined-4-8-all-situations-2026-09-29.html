Joined brackets of 4–8: every size split and every finishing order

Your set-up: V0 joined with V1, V2 fenced alone, and V3 joined with V4. Each joined bracket has two categories and 4–8 fencers. That gives 25 size splits and 486 finishing orders, and all of them were scored. Each result is compared with what the same fencer would have scored in a bracket of their own category alone.

1 · What was scored

  • The rule. Everyone scores EVF for their place in the joined bracket. A fencer beaten by at least one fencer of the younger category adds their own-category points: 3/2/1 in a category of 1–3, or EVF(K, m) in a category of 4+. Every fencer ends at least 1 point below anyone who finished ahead of them.
  • "Own bracket" is the comparison. It is N − p + 1 for a category of 1–3 (3/2/1), and EVF for a category of 4 or more, which is a real bracket.
  • Coverage. Every order of the two categories in the bracket is counted once, 3,492 fencer results in all. This includes every worst case. It treats both categories as equally strong, so the shares below are neither optimistic nor pessimistic about who wins.
  • V0 + V1 and V3 + V4 behave identically. Only "younger" and "older" matter: V0 and V3 are the younger sides, V1 and V4 the older.

Both 4+Only 4 + 4 fits within 8. Both sides can lose: −72.21 for the younger side, −57.95 for the older.
The finding

Every loss involves a category of 4 or more, which is a category that already has a real EVF bracket of its own. A category of 1–3 never loses anything by joining under this rule, whichever side it is on.

Brackets of 7 and 8 always contain a category of 4+, because 3 + 3 = 6 is the largest bracket two small categories can make.

5 · What this says about the rule

  • For two small categories, the rule does what you asked, in every order. The older side always gains, by at least 1 point, and the younger side never loses. The premium it needs is tiny: 1.0–1.9 on average, 3 at most. Most of the gain is plain EVF. A category of 1–3 scores only 3/2/1 in its own bracket, so any joined bracket beats that.
  • Your assumption that younger fencers always want to join holds when the younger category has 1–3 fencers. With 4+, it has its own EVF bracket. In orders where older fencers beat it, it scores less: on average −2 to −9 for splits of 5+1 and larger, with a worst case of −72.21. It holds even less for an older category of 4+.
  • The only large premiums (average 13–18, up to 58.9) belong to an older category of 4+, where own-category points are EVF(K, m). The 1-point rule always trims them below the fencers who beat them.
  • These losses come from rules 1 and 3 themselves, not from a parameter. No weight or premium size removes them without scoring someone above a fencer who beat them.

6 · Question: what to do with a category of 4 or more?

This replaces the question on the new-approach page
  1. Join automatically only when both categories have 1–3 fencers (brackets of 4–6)recommended
    • Nobody ever scores below their own bracket, and the older side always gains.
    • A category of 4+ fences its own bracket. It joins only if it chooses to, and the annex says in one sentence that joining can then cost points.
    • In the real events: 20 of 40 V0 + V1 joins and 9 of 30 V3 + V4 joins qualify automatically. The rest would need the category of 4+ to opt in.
  2. Join brackets of 4–8 regardless and accept the losses in §3–§4. They fall on the category of 4+: about 6% of its fencers when it is the older side and about 26% when it is the younger side, with a worst case of −72.
  3. Protect a category of 4+ with its own-bracket score as a floor. No fencer loses, but a fencer of that category can then score above fencers who beat them. That breaks the "never above anyone who beat you" rule for them.

After your answer, the implementation plan is rewritten around the rule and this decision.
